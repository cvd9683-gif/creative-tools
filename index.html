Hi, I'm Cathy. This project is about the moment before anything exists: going from nothing to a first rough version. That's zero to one. Getting from one to a hundred, refining something that already exists, is where tools like Figma, After Effects, Ableton and Procreate are excellent. I kept starting in different places: performing motion, pulling pieces out of songs I love, sketching wireframes on paper. Each time, the hard part was the same: getting started. And each time, the first version came from doing something with my hands, not from planning it.

Three things guide every idea in this project. First, zero to one: I'm designing for the first rough version, not the polishing, because tools like Figma, After Effects, Ableton and Procreate already do that well. Second, start by doing: each idea begins with a gesture, like a sketch, a performed movement or a song you love, instead of a blank document and a plan. Third, the person makes it: AI can recognize what you drew or notice patterns, but it never makes the work for you. Success means someone gets to a first version they actually want to keep developing, sooner than they would today.

Idea 1 · UX · leaning here

Your sketch becomes the prototype

WHAT IT DOES

Paper is the fastest way to sketch an app, but the sketch goes flat on screen. One photo makes it clickable, still hand-drawn.

1

Sketch screens, with arrows between them

2

Take a photo

3

Boxes become buttons, arrows become links

LATER: AR

Touch a box on the paper itself to open the next screen.

Here's the one I'm leaning toward. Paper is still the fastest way to sketch an app, but once the sketch reaches a screen it becomes a flat picture and you start over in Figma. Here you sketch a few screens on paper, with arrows showing where things go, and take one photo. The tool turns your boxes into buttons and your arrows into links, so you can tap through your idea in minutes, and it still looks like your sketch. Today people use paper prototypes, Balsamiq or go straight to Figma. Later, the AR version: a camera over your desk, and touching a box you drew opens the next screen. The AI's only job is reading what's on the page. It never draws or designs for you. Next: who it's for, why, and how I'd build it.

Idea 2 · Music

Break a song into pieces you keep

WHAT IT DOES

1

Drop in a song you love

2

It splits into stems: vocals, drums, bass, melody

3

Cut any part into a clip

4

Pin clips to boards, like Pinterest for sound

TODAY

Stem splitters, Ableton, Splice. The gap: nowhere to collect what you find.

The second idea is for music. I often start from a song I love. Here you drop in that song and it splits into stems: vocals, drums, bass and melody. You sweep across a stem to cut out the part you like, and that clip gets pinned to a board, like Pinterest but for sound. You can have a board for drums, one for late-night sounds, one for voices. Stem splitters, Ableton and Splice already exist, but nothing gives you one place to collect what you find and come back to it when you start something new. Next slide: who it's for, why, and how I'd build it.

WHY

Good sounds get lost.

Isolating one part of a song takes hours, and saved clips get scattered across apps and folders.

NEW ABILITY

Collect sounds the way Pinterest collects images, sorted by feel.

HOW

What I know

  • Tone.js cuts and plays the clips
  • Boards saved in a simple database

Still unknown

  • How fast splitting can be per song
  • Copyright: boards start private
Who: me. I start from songs I love, and then I lose the bits I liked across voice memos and folders. [Add the name of a friend who makes beats or DJs and one sentence about how they sample.] More broadly: producers, DJs, anyone who collects sounds. Why: good sounds get lost. Pulling one part out of a song takes a long time, and the clips you save end up scattered. The new ability is collecting sounds the way Pinterest collects images, sorted by how they feel. How: you upload a song in the browser. Demucs, an open-source separation model, splits it into stems on a small server. Tone.js, built on the Web Audio API, cuts and plays the clips, and boards are saved in a simple database. Unknowns: how fast splitting can be for each song, and copyright, so boards would start out private.

Idea 3 · Motion · built

Perform it, then compare takes

WHAT IT DOES

1

Pick an object in the scene

2

Press R and perform the movement with your mouse or hand

3

Compare takes, or say “longer pause”

TODAY

After Effects, Rive, Jitter. The gap: feeling it out before keyframes.

The third idea already exists as a working prototype, Motion Takes. You pick an object in a small UX scene, press R, and perform its movement with your mouse, or your hand on your own computer. The tool keeps your pauses and speed changes exactly, so you can record several takes and compare them as ghosts, then retime one by saying things like "longer pause". Motion designers usually work in After Effects, Rive or Jitter, and Adobe Character Animator already does performance-based character animation. The gap is trying out how something should feel before committing to keyframes. If there's time, I can show it live. Next: who it's for, why, and how it's built.

More broadly: motion, UI and game designers.

WHY

Keyframes want timing before you feel it.

Trying three versions of one pause means rebuilding curves three times, so people settle on the first.

NEW ABILITY

Act out a motion, keep its exact timing, compare takes.

HOW

Already built

  • Browser canvas, cursor or MediaPipe hand
  • Records position with timestamps
  • Replays by elapsed time: pauses stay
  • Takes saved locally, export as JSON

Still unknown

  • Clean keyframes for After Effects
  • Voice edits beyond a few commands
Who: me. I usually know how a motion should feel before I can build it with keyframes. [Add the name of a motion design classmate and one sentence about how they work.] More broadly: motion, UI and game designers. Why: keyframes ask you to decide the timing before you've felt it, and trying three versions of one pause means rebuilding the curves three times, so people tend to settle on the first one. The new ability is acting out a motion, keeping its exact timing and comparing takes. How: this one is already built. It runs in the browser on a canvas and records your cursor, or your hand through MediaPipe on your own computer, with timestamps. It replays by elapsed time, so pauses stay exact, and takes are saved locally and export as JSON. Unknowns: turning takes into clean keyframes for After Effects or Lottie, and voice editing beyond a few simple commands.

Here are all three side by side, so the choice is easier to see. Sketch to prototype has the clearest everyday use and it's where I'm leaning, but it has the hardest technical unknowns, especially AR. Sample start solves a real collecting problem, but music tools are crowded and copyright is a question. Motion Takes is the furthest along since it already works, but its audience is the narrowest.
