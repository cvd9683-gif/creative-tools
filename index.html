Hi, I'm Cathy. This project is about the moment before anything exists: going from nothing to a first rough version. That's zero to one. Getting from one to a hundred, refining something that already exists, is where tools like Figma are excellent. For me, that first version almost always starts on paper: I sketch screens in a notebook. So my project is sketch to prototype: your paper sketch, one photo, and a prototype you can click through.

WHAT I'M DESIGNING FOR

Zero to one: the rough first version

Start by doing: pen on paper first

You make it: it reads, never redesigns

Here's the problem. Paper is still the fastest way to think through an app: you can draw five screens in the time it takes to set up one Figma file. But the moment you want to test even one tap, you have to redraw everything digitally. By the time it's clickable, the idea has been polished before anyone has tested it, and it doesn't look like your hand anymore. People use paper prototypes, Balsamiq or go straight to Figma, but nothing carries the paper itself forward. So here's what I'm designing for. Zero to one: the rough first version, not the polish, because Figma already does one to a hundred well. Start by doing: it begins with a pen on paper, not a blank file. And you make it: the computer reads your sketch, but it never redesigns it. Success means someone gets to a first version they want to keep working on, sooner than they would today.

Here's what it does. Paper is still the fastest way to sketch an app, but once the sketch reaches a screen it becomes a flat picture and you start over in Figma. Here you sketch a few screens on paper, with arrows showing where things go, and take one photo. The tool turns your boxes into buttons and your arrows into links, so you can tap through your idea in minutes, and it still looks like your sketch. Today people use paper prototypes, Balsamiq or go straight to Figma. Later, the AR version: a camera over your desk, and touching a box you drew opens the next screen. The AI's only job is reading what's on the page. It never draws or designs for you. Next: what that looks like step by step.

User flow

From notebook to shareable link

1 · SNAP

Photograph your notebook page

2 · READ

It finds screens, boxes and arrows

3 · FIX

Drag a box or redraw a link it missed

4 · PLAY

Tap through your own drawing

5 · SHARE

Send a link to get feedback

Here's the user flow. One: you open the web app on your phone and photograph your notebook page. Two: it reads the page, finds the screens, the boxes and the arrows, and shows you what it found. Three: you fix anything it got wrong, dragging a box into place or redrawing a link it missed. This is the step that keeps you the author. Four: you tap through your own drawing as a working prototype. Five: you send a link so someone else can tap through it and give you feedback while the idea is still rough.

How I'd build it

The tech, layer by layer

LAYERWHAT HAPPENSTECHWHERE I AM
CAPTURE Snap the page with your phone HTML, CSS, JavaScript; phone camera through getUserMedia ● Comfortable
READ Find screens, boxes and arrows, and name them OpenCV.js finds the shapes; a vision API (e.g. Claude) labels them as JSON. It only reads. ● Learning
CHECK You fix anything it misread Web editor on an HTML canvas: drag boxes, redraw arrows ● Comfortable
SAVE Keep projects, share a link Firebase: Firestore for screens and links, Storage for photos ● Learning
PLAY Click through your own drawing HTML/CSS player: photo crops with clickable hotspots ● Comfortable
LATER: AR Touch the paper to open the next screen MindAR.js or Unity AR Foundation to track the page; MediaPipe Hands for your fingertip ● Unknown
Here's the tech, layer by layer. Capture: it's a web app in HTML, CSS and JavaScript, and it uses your phone camera through the browser's getUserMedia, so there's nothing to install. Read: OpenCV.js runs in the browser and finds the rectangles and lines on the page. Then a vision API, for example Claude, labels each box as a button, an image or text, and sends that back as JSON. It only reads; it never draws. Check: an editor on an HTML canvas sits on your photo, and you drag boxes or redraw arrows to fix anything it got wrong, so you stay the author. Save: Firebase. Firestore holds each project's screens and links, and Storage holds the photos, so you can share a link. Play: the prototype is just HTML and CSS: crops of your photo with clickable hotspots on top. Later, AR: MindAR.js in the browser, or Unity with AR Foundation, to track the page under a camera, and MediaPipe Hands to see when your fingertip touches a box. The data model is simple: a project has screens, and each screen has hotspots, which are a box plus the screen it links to. I'm comfortable with HTML, CSS and the player; I'm learning OpenCV, the vision API and Firebase; and the AR touch detection is the real unknown. My first build is two screens and one arrow, working end to end.

What would actually help me

I'm after stories about how you really work, not votes.

Think of the last app or interface you sketched. What happened to that sketch?

When you go from paper to screen today, what gets lost?

Would you rather tap through it on your phone, or on the paper itself?

What would make you stop using this after the first try?

These questions are meant to get real stories, not opinions, because people are better at describing what they actually did than predicting what they'd like. Question one tells me whether sketches really die, or whether people already have a way through. Question two tells me what matters most to keep: the speed, the look, or the thinking. Question three tells me whether AR is worth chasing or whether the phone is enough. Question four shows me the deal-breakers before I build. I'll write down the exact moments people describe, especially the tools they name and where they stall.
