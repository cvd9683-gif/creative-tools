Hi, I'm Cathy. This project is about the moment before anything exists: going from nothing to a first rough version. That's zero to one. Getting from one to a hundred, refining something that already exists, is where tools like Figma, After Effects, Ableton and Procreate are excellent. I kept starting in different places: performing motion, digging for music samples, scribbling, sketching wireframes. Each time, the hard part was the same: getting started. And each time, the first version came from doing something with my hands, not from planning it.

Three things guide every idea in this project. First, zero to one: I'm designing for the first rough version, not the polishing, because tools like Figma, After Effects, Ableton and Procreate already do that well. Second, start by doing: each idea begins with a gesture, like a scribble, a sketch, a performed movement or a found sound, instead of a blank document and a plan. Third, the person makes it: AI can recognize what you drew or notice patterns, but it never makes the work for you. Success means someone gets to a first version they actually want to keep developing, sooner than they would today.

Four directions, one for each place I started. Sketch to prototype is for UX and product designers: you photograph paper wireframes and tap through them. Paper prototyping is already standard practice, and existing sketch-to-design tools polish your sketch into generic UI; this keeps it yours. Sample start is for producers: chop one found sound and build from it, since the empty session is a well-known block, though music tools are crowded. Motion Takes is built: you perform how something moves and compare takes. Chaos start is the most purely zero-to-one: scribble freely, then frame what's worth keeping. It's closer to play and learning. I'm leaning toward sketch to prototype because it has the clearest real-world use.

Idea 1 · UX · leaning here

Your sketch becomes the prototype

Paper is the fastest way to sketch an app, but the sketch goes flat on screen. One photo makes it clickable, still hand-drawn.

1

Sketch screens, with arrows between them

2

Take a photo

3

Boxes become buttons, arrows become links

LATER: AR

Touch a box on the paper itself to open the next screen.

Here's the one I'm leaning toward. Paper is still the fastest way to sketch an app, but once the sketch reaches a screen it becomes a flat picture and you start over in Figma. Here you sketch a few screens on paper, with arrows showing where things go, and take one photo. The tool turns your boxes into buttons and your arrows into links, so you can tap through your idea in minutes, and it still looks like your sketch. It's for UX and product designers; today people use paper prototypes, Balsamiq or go straight to Figma. Later, the AR version: a camera over your desk, and touching a box you drew opens the next screen. The AI's only job is reading what's on the page. It never draws or designs for you.

TODAY

The second idea is for producers. A common block is the empty session: a blank project with no sound in it. Many producers start by digging for one sample. Here you drop in any sound, like a voice memo, a record or street noise, sweep to a slice you like, and it splits into pads you can tap a beat from. Ableton Live, MPC samplers and Splice already cover sampling and libraries, so this would need a sharp angle: starting from your own found sounds rather than a library.

Idea 3 · Motion · built

Perform it, then compare takes

1

Pick an object in the scene

2

Press R and perform the movement with your mouse or hand

3

Compare takes, or say “longer pause”

TODAY

After Effects, Rive, Jitter. The gap: feeling it out before keyframes.

The third idea already exists as a working prototype, Motion Takes. You pick an object in a small UX scene, press R, and perform its movement with your mouse, or your hand on your own computer. The tool keeps your pauses and speed changes exactly, so you can record several takes and compare them as ghosts, then retime one by saying things like "longer pause". Motion designers usually work in After Effects, Rive or Jitter, and Adobe Character Animator already does performance-based character animation. The gap is trying out how something should feel before committing to keyframes. If there's time, I can show it live.

Idea 4 · Illustration

Find the idea inside the mess

1

Scribble for 60 seconds, with no undo

2

Move a frame across the mess

3

Keep what you find as your first sketch

TODAY

Drawing prompts, Inktober, automatic drawing. The gap: tools reward clean marks.

The fourth idea is the most purely zero to one. Many tools reward clean marks: undo, erase, snap to grid. Here you scribble for a minute with no undo, then move a frame across the mess until something catches your eye, and you keep that as your first sketch. It's related to drawing prompts like Inktober and to the Surrealists' automatic drawing. It's closer to play and learning than industry, so it may fit better as a warm-up inside another tool.

What would actually help me

I'm after stories about how you really work, not votes. Each answer tells me what to build first.

Think of the last thing you started. What did you do first, and where did you stall?

What do you use today to get started, and what's annoying about it?

Which idea would you try on real work this week? What would make you stop?

If I can build only one in the next few weeks, which should it be, and why?

These questions are meant to get real stories, not opinions, because people are better at describing what they actually did than predicting what they'd like. Question one tells me where the real stuck moment is. Question two tells me what I'm competing with and what's broken about it. Question three separates "that's cool" from "I'd actually use it", and the "what would make you stop" part shows me the deal-breakers. Question four forces a choice, and the "why" is what I'll design from. I'll write down the specific moments people describe, especially the tools they name and the exact point where they stall.
